Our story

The poor are not
a market.

They are neighbours.

Everyone is searching for the same thing — something that doesn't move. Christian or atheist, president or peasant, the plumber and the person he can't afford to pay: all of us are looking for somewhere secure to stand.

People can't be that fixed point for each other; we change, we fail, we leave. But we still need each other. So you build something a person can turn to the moment they feel lost — and you fill it with the one thing that doesn't run out. Not an app. Each other.

The whole of the Law is two commandments: love God with everything you are, and love your neighbour as yourself. I've come to believe those are one act, not two. When you give to the poor, you give to God. Charity is just love with its sleeves rolled up — the coat off your back, the food from your plate, an hour you didn't have to give. And when the person you helped wants to give something back, you let them. Iron sharpens iron. Nobody here is only a giver, and nobody is only a case.

That's the whole idea, and it isn't new. It's in Acts, chapter four: a community that held everything in common, so that there was not a needy person among them. Not a program the state ran for them. Them — for each other.

{# Pull-quote — the line that carries the mission #}

I built the first version of this because I was tired of watching love get turned into logistics — good people ready to help, and no simple, dignified way for the help to find the need. The tools that existed treated the people they served as data to be moved, or as a market to be reached. I didn't want a better version of that. I wanted the opposite of it.

So the rule underneath everything we build is consent, not surveillance. Your community's information stays in your community's hands. Names and phone numbers are never a public list. The software is designed so that, when someone asks to be forgotten, it can actually forget them. That isn't a feature we bolted on. It's the reason the thing exists.

We started with a parish notice-board because you have to start with something real — something you can point at and say, here, look, it works. A neighbour asks for a lift to the clinic. Someone across the street can give it. A coordinator you actually know makes the introduction. That's it. That's the seed. Everything else we hope to grow — education, legal help, gardens and workshops a community owns together — grows out of that same soil, and only after it's real.
